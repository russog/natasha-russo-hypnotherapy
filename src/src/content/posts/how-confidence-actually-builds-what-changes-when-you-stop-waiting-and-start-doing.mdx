Confidence is often described as something we need before taking the next step.

A sense of certainty. A feeling of readiness. A point where things feel clear enough to move forward.

However, in practice, it doesn’t always work that way.

That sense of certainty may not arrive as expected, and so progress becomes delayed, not because the next step is impossible, but because it doesn’t yet feel quite right.

**When ability and feeling don’t match**

It is often assumed that confidence should naturally follow competence.

If you’re capable, you should feel sure. If you know what you’re doing, you should feel ready.

But these don’t always move together.

A person can be prepared, knowledgeable and experienced, yet still feel unsure at the point where action is required.

That can be confusing, particularly when there’s no obvious reason to question their ability.

The difficulty is not necessarily “I can’t.”

It may be “I don’t feel sure enough to act.”

And when feeling certain becomes a condition for moving forward, someone can find themselves waiting for reassurance that their existing ability doesn’t seem to provide.

**Confidence develops through experience**

Confidence can develop through what we do, rather than always being something we need before we begin.

Not simply by thinking things through repeatedly, but by engaging with a situation, even when it feels uncertain.

Speaking when something isn’t fully formed. Making a decision without resolving every possible outcome. Trying something without knowing exactly how it will go.

These moments may not feel like confidence at the time, but they create opportunities to learn from experience.

This isn’t always straightforward. There can still be hesitation, anticipation or the pull to wait a little longer.

But those feelings don’t necessarily mean that something has gone wrong or that a person isn’t capable of taking the next step.

**The first changes can be behavioural**

Sometimes, what changes first is not how confident you feel, but what you do.

You move a little sooner. You spend less time going back over things. You become more willing to act without feeling completely sure.

Nothing dramatic, just a shift in how you move from thinking to doing.

This is an important principle in cognitive behavioural work. Rather than waiting for a feeling to change before trying something different, behaviour can provide an opportunity to test expectations and develop new learning.

That doesn’t mean pushing yourself into every uncomfortable situation. It means considering whether a manageable step is possible without first needing to eliminate all uncertainty.

**Familiarity reduces friction**

As situations are approached rather than continually delayed, they can become more familiar.

And familiarity can change how something is experienced.

Not because everything is known, but because it is no longer entirely new.

What once required considerable effort may gradually begin to feel more manageable.

When action is postponed, we may be left relying largely on imagined outcomes. When action happens, there is an opportunity to compare those expectations with what actually occurs.

Not everything goes perfectly.

But experience can help clarify what you can handle, what may need adjusting and what you might do differently next time.

That information can influence what you expect when a similar situation arises again.

**What shifts over time**

The changes are often subtle.

There may be less hesitation around familiar situations, fewer things put off and a greater willingness to try, even when the outcome isn’t guaranteed.

This doesn’t mean uncertainty disappears. It means that feeling uncertain doesn’t always have to determine what happens next.

Confidence isn’t necessarily the absence of doubt, and it doesn’t require every experience to turn out well.

Sometimes, it develops through discovering that you can take a step, respond to what happens and learn from the experience, even without feeling completely ready beforehand.
