Sometimes the mind becomes caught on a single thought, and instead of moving on, that same thought keeps returning again and again, as if thinking about it a little longer might finally provide certainty.

We may begin examining a situation repeatedly - replaying what happened, imagining different possibilities, or looking for reassurance that nothing has been missed.

What began as an ordinary moment of thinking can gradually become a cycle of analysing and questioning.

That is often the point where thinking becomes overthinking.

**Thinking vs. overthinking**

Obviously, thinking itself is not a problem.

It is one of our most useful abilities.

Thinking helps us reflect on situations, consider options, and decide what to do next.

In many cases, thinking moves us forward.

We consider the issue, reach a conclusion, and then turn our attention elsewhere.

Overthinking tends to look different.

Instead of moving towards a decision, the mind becomes focused on trying to resolve uncertainty.

The same questions and thoughts are examined and re-examined repeatedly in an effort to make sure nothing has been missed, and no mistakes have been made.

**When the same thoughts keep returning**

Overthinking often takes the form of thoughts that keep returning.

Our minds may revisit a conversation, reconsider a decision, or imagine numerous different possibilities about what might happen next.

The same concerns are examined again and again, each time in the hope that a clear answer will appear.

You might find yourself asking questions such as:

- Did I make the right choice?
- What if something goes wrong?
- What if I overlooked something important?

Because many of our concerns involve uncertainty, the thinking rarely reaches a satisfying conclusion.

Instead, the questions return.

**Recognising the Pattern**

One useful way to recognise overthinking is to notice whether your thinking is moving towards a resolution or is circling the same ground.

Thinking usually narrows toward a decision or a next step.

Overthinking, on the other hand, often expands, generating more questions without bringing the mind any closer to a clear answer.

Importantly, this does not mean something is wrong with the mind.

In many cases, overthinking reflects an understandable attempt to prevent mistakes or gain clarity about situations that matter.

The difficulty is that the more the mind tries to resolve these questions completely, the more the thinking can begin to repeat itself.

And this raises an important question: if overthinking can feel so repetitive and exhausting, why can it be so difficult to stop?
