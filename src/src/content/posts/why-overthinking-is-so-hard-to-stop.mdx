Many people try to stop overthinking by telling themselves to “just stop worrying”.

But if it were that simple, most people would have solved the problem already.

One reason overthinking can be difficult to stop is that it often feels as though something important is being worked out. You may be trying to make sense of a conversation, reach a decision or prepare for something that could happen.

Thinking can feel productive, even when it becomes tiring. And that is part of what makes it so easy to return to.

**When thinking stops moving forward**

Thinking itself isn’t the problem. It helps us plan, make decisions, solve problems and learn from experience.

The difference isn’t necessarily how much time we spend thinking, but what the thinking is achieving.

Helpful thinking generally moves us towards a clearer understanding, a decision or something we can act on.

Overthinking, on the other hand, often involves returning to questions that cannot be fully resolved, particularly when we’re trying to eliminate uncertainty.

You might have already considered the available information, but still find yourself going back over the same possibilities. Perhaps another round of thinking will reveal something you’ve missed, or help you feel more certain about what to do.

The difficulty is that there may be nothing further to work out at that moment. Yet the thinking continues because it still feels as though an answer is just out of reach.

**The pull of “what if” thinking**

This becomes particularly noticeable when our attention moves towards things that haven’t happened yet.

What if I make the wrong decision?

What if something goes wrong later?

What if I’ve overlooked something important?

Considering possible outcomes can be useful when it helps us prepare or make a decision. But when the aim becomes ruling out every possibility of something going wrong, the questions can multiply.

One imagined outcome leads to another. You consider how you might respond to a particular problem, only to find yourself wondering what would happen if that response didn’t work.

Before long, you’re trying to find answers to situations that may never happen.

And because the future cannot be predicted with complete certainty, there is rarely a point at which every question has been settled.

**The hidden reward of overthinking**

Another reason the pattern can persist is that thinking sometimes brings a brief sense of relief.

You might reach a conclusion and think, “Okay, I’ve thought this through now.”

For a while, the tension eases. You feel a little more prepared, or reassured that you’ve considered what matters.

But then another possibility comes to mind. Something you hadn’t accounted for, or a different way the situation could unfold.

The uncertainty returns, and so does the urge to think it through again.

This is where the pattern can become self-reinforcing. If thinking occasionally reduces discomfort, even temporarily, it can become the response you turn to whenever uncertainty arises.

The problem isn’t that thinking never helps.

It’s that the relief it sometimes provides can encourage further thinking, even when no new information is being gained.

**Why trying harder doesn’t always help**

When something matters deeply to us, it’s understandable that we want to think it through carefully.

But if the difficulty is uncertainty rather than a lack of information, putting more effort into thinking may not provide the answer we’re looking for.

You can analyse a decision repeatedly without being able to guarantee its outcome. You can prepare for a difficult conversation without knowing exactly how the other person will respond.

And you can revisit something that has already happened without ever knowing precisely what someone else was thinking.

At some point, the question is no longer simply whether you’ve thought enough. It’s whether further thinking is likely to help.

That can be uncomfortable to recognise, particularly when stepping away from the problem feels like leaving something unfinished.

**Changing how we respond to the thinking**

In cognitive behavioural work, one useful starting point is to understand what the repeated thinking is trying to achieve and what happens when we engage with it.

Is there a practical problem that needs solving? Is more information genuinely available? Or are we returning to the same question because we haven’t yet reached a feeling of certainty?

Those distinctions matter.

If there is something useful to do, thinking can help us identify the next step. But when a question cannot be resolved at that moment, continuing to analyse it may simply keep the cycle going.

Changing this pattern doesn’t necessarily mean trying to stop thoughts from appearing. It may involve recognising the urge to analyse something again, without automatically following it into another round of questions.

That can feel unfamiliar at first, especially when thinking has become the usual way of responding to uncertainty.

Overthinking is difficult to step away from partly because it can feel like the responsible thing to do. Yet being willing to leave some questions unresolved can create room for a different response, rather than continuing to search for an answer that may not be available.
