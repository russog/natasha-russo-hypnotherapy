Feeling stuck can be confusing…

You might find yourself thinking things through, preparing yourself, or trying to handle things differently.

And it can look like you’re doing everything you should be doing.

But things don’t seem to shift.

Often, this happens alongside patterns like overthinking, checking, or trying to get things exactly right — all of which can feel useful, but don’t always lead to change.

What is less obvious is that many of these responses share something in common: they create a distance from what feels uncomfortable, uncertain, or difficult.

And in that way, they can quietly keep things in the same place, even while it feels like effort is being made.

**Waiting until things feel clear**

Often, this looks like a pull to feel more certain, more prepared, or more confident before taking a step.

So, you give yourself more time.

More thinking.

More planning.

More time to get it right.

And on the surface, this can feel responsible.

But when action depends on reaching that sense of clarity, it can keep getting delayed.

Not because the step isn’t possible, but because the conditions for starting keep shifting.

Over time, this can begin to act as a form of avoidance, even though it doesn’t feel like avoidance.

<u>Key point</u>: Sometimes, it’s only after starting that clarity begins to develop, rather than before.

**When thinking takes the place of doing**

At times, effort goes into thinking rather than doing.

Working something out, weighing up options, or mentally preparing can all feel like progress.

And in a way, they are — but only up to a point.

If nothing follows from that thinking, things tend to stay where they are.

Thinking without acting can also create some distance from the uncertainty or discomfort that comes with taking a step.

In that sense, it becomes a way of staying engaged without fully moving forward.

<u>Key point</u>: Allowing a small action to happen alongside that uncertainty often shifts things more than further thinking.

**Trying to do it properly**

Sometimes the difficulty isn’t starting, but the standard being set.

Often, this looks like a quiet rule: ‘If I can’t do this properly, I won’t do it at all’.

This can lead to waiting for the right time, the right conditions, or the right level of confidence.

It often comes from wanting to do things well.

But it can also delay beginning.

When something ‘must’ be done “properly,” it can become harder to approach, and easier to put off.

Over time, this can function as another way of avoiding the discomfort of starting imperfectly.

<u>Key point</u>: Allowing something to be done well enough can create movement, whereas waiting keeps things still.

**Looking for reassurance**

When something feels uncertain, it’s natural to want to check.

Looking things up, asking someone you trust, or going back over a decision can all bring a brief sense of relief.

But when this becomes something that happens repeatedly, it can have an unintended effect.

Instead of settling things, it keeps the question active.

Each check suggests that certainty still needs to be confirmed.

In this way, reassurance can become a way of trying to manage discomfort in the moment, while keeping the uncertainty going underneath.

<u>Key point</u>: Letting a question remain open, even briefly, can feel uncomfortable, but it also allows a different response to develop.

**Staying in motion without moving forward**

It’s possible to feel active without moving in a clear direction.

Starting things, shifting between ideas, or preparing multiple options can all create a sense of movement.

But if things are frequently started and not carried through, that movement doesn’t always lead anywhere.

Sometimes this reflects uncertainty about where to land.

Other times, it can be another way of staying just short of completion, where something becomes real and open to evaluation.

In that sense, it can act as a way of avoiding the discomfort that comes with finishing.

<u>Key point</u>: Letting something reach a natural end point, even if imperfect, often creates a clearer sense of direction.

**When discomfort becomes a signal to stop**

Many of these patterns have something in common.

They are ways of responding to discomfort by stepping back from it.

Waiting until something feels easier.

Trying to remove doubt before acting.

Holding back until confidence is higher.

These responses are understandable.

But, when discomfort consistently leads to pausing or delaying, it can begin to shape what gets done and what doesn’t.

In this way, discomfort becomes something that determines direction.

<u>Key point</u>: At times, it can be useful to notice what happens when a step is taken with some discomfort still present, rather than waiting for it to pass.

**When it starts to feel fixed**

Over time, these patterns can begin to feel familiar.

You might find yourself thinking:

This is just how I am.

I have always been like this.

When things are seen in this way, they can start to feel fixed.

Not because they are, but because they are no longer being questioned.

This can also create distance from change – not through action, but through how things are understood.

<u>Key point</u>: Seeing these patterns as something that has developed, rather than something inherent, can create more room to respond differently.

**A different way of understanding it**

At a glance, it can seem as though a lot is being done — yet nothing is really moving.

Time and energy are going somewhere, but they’re not quite reaching the point where something changes.

When you step back and look at it more closely, a pattern often begins to emerge:

Thinking instead of acting.

Waiting instead of beginning.

Checking instead of deciding.

These patterns can feel active, even productive.

But they tend to keep things just short of the step that would move things forward.

Seen in this way, the question isn’t about effort.

It’s about where that effort is landing.

And once that becomes clearer, something shifts.

The focus moves from trying to get everything in place first, to allowing a step to happen as things are.

That shift is often small, but it affects what happens next.
