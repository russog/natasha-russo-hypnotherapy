Feeling stuck can be confusing.

You might find yourself thinking things through, preparing yourself, or trying to handle things differently.

And it can look as though you’re doing everything you should be doing.

But things don’t seem to shift.

Often, this happens alongside patterns like overthinking, checking, or trying to get things exactly right — all of which can feel useful, but don’t always lead to change.

What is less obvious is that some of these responses can create a distance from what feels uncomfortable, uncertain or difficult. In that way, they can quietly keep things in the same place, even while considerable effort is being made.

**When preparation takes the place of progress**

Planning, weighing up options and mentally preparing can all be useful. They help us understand a situation and decide what to do next.

But sometimes the preparation continues long after it has stopped providing anything new.

You might keep gathering information, considering alternatives or refining an approach, hoping to reach a point where everything feels clear enough to begin.

On the surface, this can feel responsible.

Yet if nothing follows from that preparation, things tend to stay where they are.

Thinking about taking a step can also create some distance from the uncertainty or discomfort of actually taking it. You remain engaged with the problem without quite reaching the point where something changes.

The difficulty isn’t necessarily the amount of effort involved, but whether that effort is helping you move towards a decision or action.

**Trying to do it properly**

Sometimes the difficulty isn’t starting, but the standard being set.

Often, this looks like a quiet rule: “If I can’t do this properly, I won’t do it at all.”

It can come from wanting to do things well, which isn’t a problem in itself.

But when something must be done under exactly the right conditions, it can become harder to approach and easier to put off.

A task may be repeatedly adjusted, a decision reconsidered or a piece of work delayed because it doesn’t yet feel good enough.

In some situations, this can become a way of avoiding the discomfort of doing something imperfectly or having the result open to evaluation.

There is a difference between taking reasonable care and setting a standard that makes completion increasingly difficult.

Allowing something to be done well enough, where that’s appropriate, can sometimes create more progress than continually trying to improve it before it has even been finished.

**Staying in motion without moving forward**

It’s possible to feel active without moving in a clear direction.

Starting things, shifting between ideas or preparing multiple options can all create a sense of movement.

But if things are frequently started and not carried through, that activity doesn’t always lead anywhere.

Sometimes this reflects genuine uncertainty about which direction to take.

Other times, staying busy with the earlier stages can make it easier to avoid the point of completion, when a decision becomes real or the result can be evaluated.

This can be frustrating because time and energy are being used. The person isn’t necessarily doing nothing.

Yet the effort may be spread across so many preparations, revisions or unfinished tasks that little reaches a meaningful endpoint.

**Looking at where the effort is going**

In cognitive behavioural work, one useful starting point is to look at what happens just before progress slows down.

Does preparation continue even when enough information is available? Does the standard for finishing keep changing? Are new tasks being started when an existing one becomes difficult to complete?

These questions can help identify whether a particular behaviour is moving something forward or providing temporary relief from the discomfort of taking the next step.

That doesn’t mean every delay is avoidance. Sometimes more preparation is necessary, circumstances genuinely prevent progress, or a task needs to be reconsidered.

But where the same pattern keeps repeating, small behavioural changes can provide useful information. Completing one manageable part, making a decision with the information already available, or allowing something to reach a reasonable stopping point may help test whether the additional preparation was really needed.

When you’re putting in effort but still feeling stuck, it can be worth looking beyond how much you’re doing and considering where that effort is landing.

Sometimes the difference isn’t doing more. It’s allowing something you’ve already been working towards to move forward.
