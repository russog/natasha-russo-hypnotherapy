It’s not always difficult to know what you want.

You may already have a sense of what you think, what you would prefer, or what would feel right to say or do.

What tends to be harder is allowing that to come through clearly in what actually happens.

Difficulties with assertiveness often show up in small, everyday moments.

You might agree to something you’re not fully comfortable with, hold back a view even though it feels clear, or avoid asking for something you would genuinely prefer.

Individually, these moments seem manageable, but they influence how situations unfold.

**Why expressing yourself can feel difficult**

Sometimes, what makes that moment difficult is not uncertainty about what you want, but how expressing it is expected to feel.

Saying something directly can feel uncomfortable, acting on a decision can feel exposing, and expressing a preference can carry a sense of risk — of tension, disagreement, or being seen differently.

Even when those outcomes are unlikely, anticipating them can be enough to change how someone responds.

Holding back may feel easier in the moment. You avoid an uncomfortable conversation, the possibility of disagreement or the uncertainty of how someone might react.

But when something isn’t expressed clearly, others respond to what is available to them.

They move forward based on what has been said or agreed.

That’s a natural process, but it can lead to outcomes that don’t match what was originally intended.

Conversations may need to be revisited, decisions adjusted, and things can take longer to settle.

You might find yourself going along with things more than you intended, or noticing that what is happening doesn’t fully reflect what you would have chosen.

Repeated over time, this can lead to a quiet sense of frustration or being slightly out of step.

**How holding back can become a habit**

Early behavioural approaches, including the work of Andrew Salter, considered how people may inhibit responses they are capable of expressing.

From a cognitive behavioural perspective, this pattern can become reinforced over time.

When holding back reduces discomfort in the moment, that immediate relief can make the same response more likely next time.

The difficulty is that avoiding an uncomfortable response also limits opportunities to discover what might happen if you expressed yourself more directly.

That doesn’t mean every situation calls for assertiveness. There are times when choosing not to speak or act is entirely reasonable. The important distinction is whether holding back is a deliberate choice or something that increasingly happens because expressing yourself feels uncomfortable.

**Expressing yourself without waiting for certainty**

Assertiveness isn’t necessarily about feeling confident or knowing that other people will respond positively.

It involves being able to communicate your preferences, views or needs clearly and respectfully, while recognising that others may have different ones.

Sometimes that means saying what you think even when you feel hesitant.At other times, it may involve making a request or expressing a preference without knowing exactly how it will be received.

These can be small opportunities to practise responding differently, rather than automatically allowing anticipated discomfort to make the decision.

And the more clearly you express what you actually want, the more opportunity there is for what happens next to reflect it.
