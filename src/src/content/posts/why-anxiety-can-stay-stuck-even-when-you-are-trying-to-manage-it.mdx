**Anxiety doesn’t always look dramatic**

When people think of anxiety, they often imagine something obvious: panic, visible distress or a clear crisis point when everything spills over.

But anxiety doesn’t always appear like that.

For many people, it is quieter and more persistent. A constant background worry, a sense of unease or the feeling that something is always slightly “off”.

Not enough to stop you from functioning, but enough to leave you feeling mentally stretched.

From the outside, you may look completely fine. You show up, meet responsibilities, make decisions and keep things moving.

Inside, though, you may feel as though you’re constantly managing something that nobody else can see.

**How this can feel day to day**

You might find yourself anticipating problems, reviewing conversations or worrying about things that haven’t happened yet.

You continue doing what needs to be done, but ordinary situations may require more effort than other people realise.

Perhaps you manage a meeting while feeling tense throughout, or deal with an unexpected problem while privately worrying about whether you’ve handled it correctly.

Nothing particularly unusual may be visible to anyone else.

And because you get through these situations, it can be easy to overlook how much they are taking out of you.

The important distinction is that being able to do something doesn’t necessarily mean doing it feels manageable.

**Why does it often get missed?**

This is perhaps one of the more difficult aspects of anxiety that remains largely invisible.

When someone continues working, socialising and meeting their responsibilities, other people may have little reason to suspect they are struggling.

But sometimes the person experiencing the anxiety doesn’t fully recognise it either.

They tell themselves they’re fine because they’re still coping. They assume that if things were genuinely difficult, they wouldn’t be managing as well as they are.

And so the anxiety is minimised.

Over time, feeling slightly on edge, anticipating problems or pushing through discomfort can start to seem like an ordinary part of life.

The difficulty is that functioning becomes the measure of how well someone is doing, rather than how they’re actually experiencing their day.

Yet these are two different things.

You can be competent, reliable and capable of managing your responsibilities while still experiencing anxiety that deserves attention.

**When coping becomes the usual response**

Some people become accustomed to responding to anxiety by continuing with whatever needs to be done.

In many situations, that is useful. Feeling anxious doesn’t necessarily mean we need to stop, and being able to continue despite discomfort can be an important strength.

But there is a difference between continuing with life while acknowledging anxiety and repeatedly dismissing distress simply because you’re managing to function.

If the usual response is to push through without considering what is happening, there may be little opportunity to recognise patterns that are making everyday life unnecessarily difficult.

This doesn’t mean that capable people are naturally more anxious, or that functioning well is somehow a problem.

It means that outward performance doesn’t always tell us much about someone’s internal experience.

**Recognising what is happening**

In cognitive behavioural work, an important starting point is understanding how anxiety shows up for the individual, including patterns that may have become so familiar they are barely noticed.

That might involve recognising particular worries, expectations or ways of responding that contribute to the difficulty.

It also means looking beyond whether someone is getting through their day and considering how much effort, tension or distress accompanies that process.

Understanding these patterns can help identify what might need to change, without assuming that everything someone is already doing is unhelpful.

Anxiety doesn’t have to reach a crisis point before it is worth addressing.

And being able to cope on the outside doesn’t mean that what you’re experiencing on the inside should be overlooked.
