When we’re anxious, it’s natural to look for ways to feel safer, more prepared or more in control.

Sometimes that means avoiding something uncomfortable. At other times, it might involve checking, seeking reassurance or trying to anticipate everything that could go wrong.

These responses can seem sensible, particularly when they bring relief.

But sometimes, the very things we do to manage anxiety can also help keep it going.

**What are safety behaviours?**

Safety behaviours are things we do to prevent a feared outcome, reduce anxiety or protect ourselves from a situation we believe might be difficult to handle.

They are often quite ordinary and may become so familiar that we barely notice them.

You might draft and re-draft a message several times before sending it, just to avoid getting something wrong.

You might ask someone, “Do you think that was okay?”, even though you’ve already received reassurance.

Or you may avoid a situation, leave early or wait until you feel calmer before doing something you would otherwise be capable of doing.

The behaviour itself isn’t always the problem. Checking an important message or preparing for a difficult conversation can be perfectly reasonable.

What matters is the purpose the behaviour serves and whether you begin to feel that you cannot manage without it.

**Why do safety behaviours keep anxiety going?**

The difficulty is not that these behaviours are necessarily bad. It’s what can happen when we repeatedly rely on them.

Imagine someone who believes they will embarrass themselves unless they carefully rehearse everything they want to say.

They prepare extensively, have the conversation and nothing particularly embarrassing happens.

That might seem reassuring. But if they attribute the successful conversation entirely to their preparation, they may conclude that rehearsing everything was necessary.

The original fear remains largely untested.

They haven’t had much opportunity to discover whether they could manage the conversation with less preparation, or cope with an awkward moment if one occurred.

This is one way safety behaviours can maintain anxiety. They provide short-term relief while limiting opportunities to develop different expectations through experience.

And because the relief feels useful, the behaviour becomes tempting to repeat.

**When protection becomes a habit**

Over time, these responses can begin to happen almost automatically.

An uncertain situation arises, anxiety increases, and the familiar protective behaviour follows.

You check again, seek another opinion or postpone something until you feel more comfortable.

The immediate discomfort may ease, reinforcing the impression that the behaviour was necessary.

But the next time a similar situation arises, the same uncertainty may return, along with the urge to protect yourself in the same way.

This doesn’t mean every attempt to feel safer is unhelpful. Genuine precautions are important, and some situations do require preparation or additional support.The question is whether a particular behaviour is helping you respond to a real demand or has become something you rely on primarily to manage a feared possibility.

**Learning to respond differently**

In cognitive behavioural work, one useful starting point is to identify a safety behaviour and understand what someone expects might happen without it.

What is the behaviour intended to prevent? What relief does it provide? And what opportunities for learning might be missed by continuing to rely on it?

From there, it may be possible to experiment gradually with a different response in situations where doing so is appropriate and safe.

For example, someone who repeatedly checks a routine message might try sending one after a reasonable review, rather than continuing until they feel completely reassured.

The aim isn’t to force anxiety away or abandon sensible precautions. It’s to discover whether the feared outcome is as likely as expected, whether the protective behaviour is genuinely necessary and what can be managed if things don’t go perfectly.

Sometimes anxiety decreases as a result. At other times, the important learning is that uncertainty or discomfort can be handled without relying on the same protective response.

Recognising a safety behaviour doesn’t mean you have to change everything at once. But understanding what keeps a particular pattern going can create opportunities to respond differently, rather than automatically repeating what brings relief in the moment.
