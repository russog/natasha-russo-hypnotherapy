Most people think of self-consciousness as something fairly obvious.

Feeling shy. Feeling awkward. Feeling nervous socially.

But for many, it happens much more subtly than that.

Sometimes, a conversation can be flowing relatively naturally until something suddenly shifts internally.

A simple “You look nice today” or “You did that really well” can create an unexpected moment of self-consciousness. You might laugh awkwardly, minimise the compliment or suddenly become more aware of how you’re coming across.

At other times, it happens so quietly that you barely notice it.

You might be listening to someone while simultaneously monitoring your own facial expressions, tone of voice, choice of words or how the other person is responding.

Sometimes it can feel almost like having two conversations at once: the interaction itself, and the internal commentary running alongside it.

**When attention turns towards yourself**

The difficulty isn’t necessarily a lack of social ability. It’s that some of your attention is being used to observe and evaluate yourself rather than engage with the conversation.

You may start wondering whether you’ve said the right thing, whether a pause lasted too long or whether the other person noticed something awkward.

In an attempt to avoid getting things wrong, you become more careful.

Responses may feel rehearsed, pauses more noticeable, and spontaneity can gradually give way to hesitation.

The more closely you monitor yourself, the more there seems to be to monitor.

And that can make even ordinary conversations surprisingly tiring, despite nothing appearing particularly difficult from the outside.

**Why trying harder can make things feel less natural**

Self-monitoring often begins as an attempt to manage how we’re perceived.

If you’re concerned about coming across badly, paying closer attention to what you’re saying or doing can seem like a sensible precaution.

But conversations require us to respond to another person, not simply evaluate our own performance.

When too much attention is directed towards ourselves, it can become harder to follow what the other person is saying, respond spontaneously or remain engaged in the interaction.

This can create an uncomfortable cycle. The conversation feels less natural, which may be taken as a sign that something isn’t going well, leading to even more self-monitoring.

Over time, someone may begin to assume they’re simply shy, awkward or lacking confidence, when part of the difficulty may be the amount of attention they’re directing towards themselves.

**Learning to shift attention**

One useful approach in cognitive behavioural work is to explore what happens when attention is directed away from continuous self-monitoring and back towards the conversation.

That doesn’t mean forcing yourself to stop feeling self-conscious or trying to appear confident.

It might begin with something as ordinary as paying closer attention to what the other person is actually saying, rather than continually checking how your response sounds.

The aim isn’t to perform perfectly.It’s to become more involved in the interaction itself, even if some self-consciousness remains.

This may feel unfamiliar at first, particularly if monitoring yourself has become an automatic habit.

But with practice, conversations can become less about trying to manage every impression and more about responding to the person in front of you.

And that can leave more room for the spontaneity and connection that constant self-monitoring tends to interrupt.
