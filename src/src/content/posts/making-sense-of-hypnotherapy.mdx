“I’ve read your website… but I’m still not quite sure what hypnotherapy actually is.”

I can understand that. Hypnotherapy is often explained in ways that either feel vague or disappear into technical language. Neither really helps when you’re trying to understand what it involves.

So let me explain it differently.

Hypnosis involves focusing attention and engaging with suggestions that can influence what you notice, how you feel or how you respond. Hypnotherapy is the use of hypnosis for a therapeutic purpose. The two are closely connected, but they don’t mean quite the same thing.

**A familiar experience — just used deliberately**

We all use our imagination in everyday life. We replay conversations on the drive home. We picture future situations before they happen. We worry about what might go wrong or rehearse how we’ll cope if it does.

What we imagine can affect how we feel, even when the situation itself isn’t happening.

Hypnosis makes deliberate use of familiar abilities such as attention and imagination, but with something important added: suggestion.

A suggestion gives your attention a particular direction. It might invite you to imagine a sensation, notice something differently or explore another way of responding. For example, focusing on the suggestion that your hand is becoming warmer may sometimes influence how that sensation is experienced, even though nothing around you has changed.

What’s interesting is that we don’t always experience these responses as simply thinking about an idea. Sometimes a suggested experience can feel surprisingly vivid or natural, almost as though it’s happening by itself.

That doesn’t happen in the same way for everyone. Some people experience noticeable changes in sensations, feelings or responses, while others notice subtler effects. The way someone responds can also vary depending on the suggestion.

**Understanding something isn’t always the same as experiencing it**

We can understand something perfectly well in principle without that understanding necessarily changing how we feel.

Someone might know they can cope with a challenging situation, yet still feel overwhelmed by the thought of facing it.

Hypnosis can provide an opportunity to explore a different response through focused imagination and suggestion. Rather than simply telling themselves they can cope, they might imagine what coping would actually involve, including how they could respond while some of their usual nerves are still present.

But hypnosis isn’t limited to rehearsing behaviour. Suggestions can also involve changes in sensations, emotional experiences or where attention is directed.

None of this requires someone to abandon their judgement or believe something they know is false. You can engage with an imagined possibility while knowing that you’re imagining it.

And although an experience in hypnosis may feel meaningful, it doesn’t automatically produce lasting change. What happens when someone applies what they’ve explored in everyday life also matters.

**Why the word “hypnosis” causes so much confusion**

The word hypnosis carries quite a lot of cultural baggage. For many people, it brings to mind stage performances, loss of control or being “put under”.

It’s understandable that these associations create uncertainty about what hypnosis actually involves.

There are different scientific theories about how hypnosis works. Some describe it in terms of a distinctive state of consciousness, while others explain hypnotic responses through more familiar psychological processes.

In the non-state approach I practise, hypnosis doesn’t require a special trance state. Instead, the emphasis is on processes such as attention, imagination, expectation and the way we respond to suggestions.

This doesn’t mean people are pretending, or that the experience cannot feel unusual. Sometimes a response to suggestion can feel relatively automatic, even though the person is actively engaged in the process.

And importantly, hypnosis isn’t about handing over control to someone else. You remain involved, can communicate, question a suggestion, ask for it to be changed or choose to stop.

Relaxation can be part of hypnosis, but it isn’t a requirement. Someone can engage with suggestions while feeling quite alert.

**Where cognitive behavioural hypnotherapy fits**

Cognitive behavioural hypnotherapy (CBH) combines hypnosis with cognitive and behavioural approaches.

These approaches help us understand how expectations, interpretations and patterns of responding can contribute to particular difficulties, and how different responses might be developed and tested.

Hypnosis can complement that work through focused attention, imagination and suggestion. It offers another way of exploring experiences or practising responses that may be useful outside the hypnotic context.

The intention isn’t to replace practical change with imagination, but to use these psychological processes together in a purposeful way.

**But is this actually evidence-based?**

It’s a reasonable question, particularly given some of the claims made about hypnosis.

Attention, expectation, imagination and responses to suggestion have all been studied in psychological research. That research helps us understand aspects of hypnotic experience, although researchers don’t agree on every aspect of how hypnosis works.

Whether hypnotherapy is effective for a particular difficulty is a separate question, and one that needs to be answered by looking at treatment outcomes.

Research has investigated hypnosis in several clinical areas, including pain management, as well as its use alongside psychological treatments. One meta-analysis examining hypnosis added to cognitive behavioural therapy found some additional benefits, although the findings varied across the difficulties studied and the outcomes measured.

That doesn’t mean hypnosis is equally effective for everything, or that adding it to another treatment will always improve the result. Its usefulness depends on the difficulty being addressed, the way it’s used and the strength of the available evidence.

What I find helpful about the non-state understanding of hypnosis is that it removes much of the mystery without dismissing the experience itself.

It allows us to look at hypnosis as something a person actively engages in, using attention, imagination and suggestion in a deliberate way. Hypnotherapy brings those processes into therapeutic work, with a purpose that goes beyond the hypnotic experience itself.
