It is striking how quickly people can move on from things they once desperately wanted.

For a while, an achievement, a personal change, or successfully navigating a difficult period may feel significant.

Then, often without much awareness, it simply becomes part of everyday life.

Attention moves to the next challenge, the next goal, or the next thing that still needs improving.

As a result, people can find themselves moving forward, learning and growing, yet still feeling as though they are standing still.

**When Progress Quietly Becomes Invisible**

Most meaningful changes happen gradually. They develop through repetition, practice, experience and persistence.

A person who once replayed difficult conversations for days beforehand may now speak up almost automatically. Someone who once struggled to say ‘no’ may now set boundaries with relatively little thought. A situation that once felt overwhelming may now be handled without much conscious effort at all.

Over time, however, these experiences become familiar.

The difficulty is that once something becomes familiar and more automatic, it can stop feeling like progress.

Progress does not disappear. It simply becomes ordinary.

Because of this, people can easily underestimate how much they have already changed.

**“I’ll Feel Better When…”**

Alongside overlooking progress, many people also find themselves postponing feeling okay until some future condition is met.

Examples of this are surprisingly common:

- “I’ll relax when work calms down.”
- “I’ll feel confident when I have more experience.”
- “I’ll be happy when I achieve this goal.”
- “I’ll feel proud of myself when I have done a little bit more.”

Goals themselves are not the problem. They provide direction, purpose and opportunities for growth.

However, difficulties can arise when our sense of wellbeing becomes dependent upon achieving them.

A goal may be reached, bringing a brief sense of relief or accomplishment, before attention quickly moves to the next thing that still needs to happen.

The conditions for feeling okay quietly continue to shift. And when there is always another condition to meet, satisfaction can remain just out of reach.

**Different Rules for Ourselves**

Interestingly, many people would never judge somebody else in the way they judge themselves.

If a friend had achieved everything they had achieved over the previous year, they might respond with encouragement, compassion and recognition of the effort involved.

Yet when evaluating themselves, the rules often change.

Achievements are minimised.

Effort is discounted.

Attention is drawn towards mistakes, perceived shortcomings and everything that has not yet been accomplished.

The result can be a persistent sense of not quite doing enough, not quite being enough, or not quite being where one “should” be.

**What Keeps This Pattern Going?**

These experiences can reinforce one another.

When attention repeatedly moves towards what is missing or unfinished, progress becomes easier to overlook. And when personal rules determine that satisfaction is only justified after the next achievement, even genuine accomplishments may not seem to count for very long.

Over time, this can contribute to ongoing pressure, self-criticism and difficulty appreciating how far we’ve come.

**Learning to Recognise Progress**

In cognitive behavioural hypnotherapy (CBH), one area we can explore is the rules and assumptions that shape how people evaluate themselves and their progress.

Rather than simply encouraging positive thinking, this involves recognising patterns such as discounting achievements or setting conditions for feeling satisfied, and examining whether those standards are reasonable or helpful.

It can also mean deliberately giving some attention to what has changed, rather than automatically moving towards what still needs improving.

This isn’t about pretending everything is going well, overlooking genuine difficulties or abandoning personal ambitions. It’s about recognising that progress doesn’t become less meaningful simply because it has started to feel ordinary.

Goals, growth and ambition are not the problem. The difficulty arises when nothing we’ve already achieved is allowed to count.

So perhaps it’s worth asking:

When was the last time you genuinely noticed how far you have already come?
