There are moments in life that deserve our attention: a disagreement with someone we care about; a difficult decision; a genuine setback.

But then, there are much smaller moments… moments that, objectively, are not especially significant, yet somehow continue to occupy our thoughts long after they are over.

The interesting question is why.

Why do some ordinary experiences seem so difficult to let go of, while others barely leave a trace?

Part of the answer is that it’s not always the event itself that creates the greatest emotional impact. More often, it’s what we believe it says about us.

This is something that happens so naturally that it often goes unnoticed.

When an experience doesn’t sit quite right, it’s natural to reflect on it. But the difficulty is not the reflection itself. It begins when the focus quietly shifts from understanding the experience to drawing conclusions about who we are. At that point, the experience is no longer just something that happened. It has become a judgement about ourselves. That is a subtle shift, but an important one, because the interpretation now feels like a fact rather than one possible explanation.

Once that shift has taken place, other patterns often begin to appear.

One of the most common is the quiet influence of internal rules.

Many people carry expectations that have been present for so long they no longer feel like expectations at all. They simply feel like the way things should be - the belief that they should cope; that they should know what to say; they should be handling life better by now. These rules are rarely questioned because they feel obvious. Yet, they often become the standard against which people measure themselves when life doesn’t quite go to plan. The result is that disappointment with a situation can gradually become disappointment with oneself.

Once we’ve started reaching conclusions about ourselves, later experiences are rarely viewed in isolation. One difficult conversation, one mistake or one moment of uncertainty can begin to reinforce the conclusion we’ve already started believing. That conclusion starts feeling increasingly obvious, not necessarily because it’s the most accurate explanation, but because more and more experiences seem to fit it.

The way people speak to themselves after something hasn’t gone to plan can also quietly reinforce that same conclusion. Compare these two responses: _“That didn’t go as I’d hoped,”_ vs. “_I always get these things wrong”_ … those two ways of speaking to ourselves may sound similar, but over time they can lead us in very different directions. That’s often the point where an everyday experience begins carrying far more weight than it ever deserved.

None of these processes are unusual. They’re just ways of trying to make sense of experience. The difficulty is simply that they happen so quickly and so automatically that they are often mistaken for facts rather than interpretations. One ordinary moment becomes personal. A personal interpretation becomes a conclusion. Over time, that conclusion can begin to shape confidence, increase self-doubt and influence what people expect from themselves and from future situations.

One of the aims of Cognitive Behavioural Hypnotherapy is to slow that process down, not by encouraging positive thinking or dismissing difficult experiences, but by creating enough space to look at them differently. Instead of automatically accepting the first conclusion, it encourages questions such as:

Is this the only possible explanation?

What evidence am I using to reach this conclusion?

What else might explain what happened?

Am I responding to the situation itself, or to what I’ve started believing it says about me?

Sometimes, simply asking those questions is enough to interrupt the process. The situation hasn’t changed, but the meaning attached to it often does. And that can make all the difference.
