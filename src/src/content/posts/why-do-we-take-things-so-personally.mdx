There are moments in life that deserve our attention: a disagreement with someone we care about, a difficult decision, a genuine setback.

But then there are much smaller moments. Experiences that may seem relatively insignificant, yet somehow continue to occupy our thoughts long after they are over.

Why do some ordinary experiences seem so difficult to let go of, while others barely leave a trace?

Part of the answer is that it’s not always the event itself that creates the greatest emotional impact. Sometimes, it’s what we believe it says about us.

When an experience doesn’t sit quite right, it’s natural to reflect on it. But the difficulty is not the reflection itself. It begins when the focus quietly shifts from understanding the experience to drawing conclusions about who we are. At that point, the experience is no longer just something that happened. It has become a judgement about ourselves.

That is a subtle shift, but an important one, because the interpretation can begin to feel like a fact rather than one possible explanation.

One of the patterns that can contribute to this is the quiet influence of internal rules.

Many people carry expectations that have been present for so long they no longer feel like expectations at all. They simply feel like the way things should be: the belief that they should cope, that they should know what to say, that they should be handling life better by now.

These rules are rarely questioned because they feel obvious. Yet they often become the standard against which people measure themselves when life doesn’t quite go to plan. The result is that disappointment with a situation can gradually become disappointment with oneself.

Once we’ve started reaching conclusions about ourselves, later experiences are rarely viewed in isolation. One difficult conversation, one mistake or one moment of uncertainty can begin to reinforce a conclusion we’ve already started believing. That conclusion starts feeling increasingly obvious, not necessarily because it’s the most accurate explanation, but because more and more experiences seem to fit it.

The way people speak to themselves after something hasn’t gone to plan can also reinforce that same conclusion. Compare these two responses:

“That didn’t go as I’d hoped.”

“I always get these things wrong.”

Those two ways of speaking to ourselves may seem like small variations, but they carry very different meanings. The first describes an experience. The second turns that experience into a judgement about ourselves.

None of these processes are unusual. They’re ways of trying to make sense of experience. But when those interpretations go unquestioned, an ordinary moment can begin carrying far more weight than it deserves. Over time, the conclusions we draw can shape our confidence, increase self-doubt and influence what we expect from ourselves in future situations.

One of the aims of cognitive behavioural work is to slow that process down, not by encouraging positive thinking or dismissing difficult experiences, but by creating enough space to look at them differently.

Instead of automatically accepting the first conclusion, we can begin to ask:

- Is this the only possible explanation?
- What evidence am I using to reach this conclusion?
- What else might explain what happened?
- Am I responding to the situation itself, or to what I’ve started believing it says about me?

These questions won’t necessarily change how we feel straight away, particularly when a belief about ourselves has become deeply familiar. But they can help us recognise the difference between what happened and the meaning we’ve attached to it.

And that distinction matters. An experience can be disappointing, uncomfortable or difficult without automatically becoming evidence of who we are.
